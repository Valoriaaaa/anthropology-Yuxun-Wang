Description

My research interests are anchored in three interconnected domains: Information Science, heritage studies, and museology. Within Information Science, I am interested in how archival institutional mechanisms (such as acquisition, description, classification, and collection) accommodate and simultaneously reshape context-specific, embodied community memories when they enter institutional archival systems through participatory archiving practices. Does this process achieve archival justice, or does it reproduce archival injustice? In heritage studies, I examine the politics of intangible cultural heritage (ICH) documentation and how community-led conservation practices challenge top-down, state-centric models of heritage designation. In museology, I conceptualize curatorial practice as a mediating form of knowledge production, analyzing how exhibition design, object classification systems, and interpretive frameworks encode institutional power relations within cultural institutions.

Doctoral Research Agenda

The Metadatafication of Oral History in Participatory Interpretation: A Study on Museum Selection, Description, and Representation Mechanisms

Core Questions

This study examines participatory interpretation initiatives in museums situated within place-based communities, with oral history serving as the primary case study to represent community voices. The central inquiry concerns the museum's curation of oral narratives contributed by both residents and visitors. Specifically, it investigates the mechanisms through which community voices are mediated, filtered, or modified within institutional archives.

The research addresses three key questions:

  • What elements of oral history are subject to selection or alteration?
  • Why are these interventions undertaken?
  • How do various structural factors and stakeholder dynamics shape this process?

Exploration of Cultural Heritage Interpretation Pathways from the Perspective of Participatory Development Theory — A Case Study of Nanjing's "Digital Heritage" Project

Yuxun Wang (First Author) 2026 (Forthcoming) 3rd Forum on Cultural Heritage and Social Development, Zhejiang University

Abstract

The introduction of innovative change in the interpretation practices in museums is essential in cultural heritage institutions to deliver the cultural heritage in an effective manner and fulfill the social value of cultural heritage. Another benefit of exploring new methods of heritage interpretation is that it not only responds to the needs of the public to be engaged in cultural activities but encourages new forms of interpretation. Basing on the outlook of the participatory development theory, the given study incorporates its main idea, the ability to turn the passive receivers into the active creators, in the examination of the cultural heritage interpretation routes. The research with the help of case analysis suggests a three-phase model of participatory interpretation, which presents a theoretically based and practically used framework of innovation of the cultural heritage interpretation techniques. The study targets the role of the multi-stakeholder intervention in supporting the shift of heritage interpretation towards sharing process rather than delivering outcomes. A hybrid approach of participatory inquiry and case study was taken, and an intensive two-year follow-up study of the project of Digital Heritage on the Nanjing Great Bao'en Temple Site Museum was performed. The results show that the participatory development theory offers a fundamental rationale of mutual interpretive engagements in cultural heritage which highlight the transition of closed functioning towards gradual openness to the populace as well as the maintenance of the quality of interpretation by moderate delegation of cultural authority. This method provides a radical model between theory and practice of innovation in heritage interpretation.

Introduction

I initiated research on this museum project in 2024, independently designing its research framework and methodology, which ultimately formed the basis of my undergraduate thesis. Over a two-year longitudinal study, I engaged in participant observation through three overlapping roles—contestant, curator, and event staff—while collecting extensive primary data via questionnaires and semi-structured in-depth interviews with audiences and internal personnel. This empirical evidence underpinned the development of my final interpretive model.

This research systematically analyzes how multi-stakeholder engagement at distinct stages of museum object interpretation catalyzes the excavation of the objects' multi-layered meanings, and proposes a three-stage participatory interpretation model. The model posits that, within the context of cultural institutions (e.g., museums) retaining interpretive sovereignty, museum object interpretation remains a closed process for the public. This closure situates visitors in an "absent context" between the interpretive process and the exhibited display, leading to the erosion of partial cultural agency. To address this impasse, the model centers on the logic of shared interpretive processes, emphasizing a shift from closure to openness and from one-way transmission to community co-creation. It argues that diverse communities can intervene at different stages of the interpretive process, thereby mitigating the absent context.

This experience has solidified my commitment to advancing scholarship in museum anthropology. Doctoral studies will afford me the sustained, coherent timeframe necessary to systematically investigate the topics that both perplex and captivate me.

Paper Structure

Key Outcomes

Dynamic Three-Stage Model for Cultural Heritage Interpretation

This study constructs a dynamic three-stage model for cultural heritage interpretation oriented toward open cultural rights. The theoretical model draws on two foundational frameworks:

  • Jelka Pirković's General Model of Heritage Interpretation — emphasizes the co-participation of memory, values, and knowledge as core drivers of interpretation.
  • Tolkach et al.'s Community-Centered Heritage Sustainability Model — advocates for community-led sustainable heritage management enabled by multidimensional empowerment.

The model conceptualizes interpretation as an interpenetrating dynamic system of three interdependent stages:

  1. Pre-interpretation: Aggregation of interpretive resources mediated by the community — community actors curate and mobilize local heritage resources as the basis for interpretation.
  2. In-interpretation: Translation of interpretive materials by participants — participants recontextualize and transform heritage content into accessible, contextually relevant forms.
  3. Post-interpretation: Evaluation of interpretive outcomes by internal experts and external audiences — dual-stakeholder feedback validates the accuracy, relevance, and impact of interpretation.

These stages form a closed-loop system through empowerment mechanisms, driving continuous reconstruction and renewal of heritage meanings among diverse stakeholders.

Empirical findings: The model effectively enhances the public's emotional connection to heritage and cultivates a sense of ownership.

Three key challenges: (1) insufficient participation sustainability; (2) ambiguous boundaries of power devolution; (3) absence of long-term impact assessment.

Exploring Innovative Development Pathways for Small and Medium-Sized Museums from the Perspective of Youth Co-Creation — A Practice Study Based on International Competitions

Yuxun Wang 2025 2025 International Museum Day Youth Academic Forum (ICOM Jiangsu)

Abstract

Against the backdrop of rapid social transformation and development, museums are confronting unprecedented opportunities and challenges. As a critical component of the museum ecosystem, small and medium-sized museums (SMMs) are also grappling with multiple predicaments, including limited collections, suboptimal exhibition quality, and a scarcity of professional curatorial talent. Addressing these issues has emerged as a top priority in contemporary museum practice.

Youth groups, as the core driving force behind social change, hold central significance for alleviating the dilemmas faced by SMMs—researching youth communities and gaining insights into their thoughts and needs is therefore academically and practically imperative. Thus, implementing the concept of youth co-creation in museums has become a pivotal strategy for museum development. Youth co-creation encompasses diverse pathways, such as museum-school partnerships, competition organization, and volunteer recruitment. However, there remains untapped potential in the development of museum-centric competitions.

Drawing on the author's first-hand participation in China's inaugural international museum competition, Eternal Heritage: Immersive Cultural Heritage Maker Challenge, this study adopts a participatory observation approach to examine how such events, under the framework of youth co-creation, facilitate the construction of interdisciplinary teams, expand international perspectives, integrate cutting-edge technologies, and embed authentic youth viewpoints. Concurrently, these competitions provide platforms and opportunities for youth development, fostering a mutually reinforcing virtuous cycle that offers novel insights into resolving the current predicaments of SMMs.

The study concludes that international museum competitions effectively drive youth engagement in museum co-creation, serving as a viable and effective pathway to address the challenges faced by SMMs. This approach is therefore worthy of broader promotion and practical application in the SMM sector.

InteractiMuse — Virtual Museum Website

AdventureX 2026 Competition — Award-Winning Project

A virtual museum website project that won awards across three tracks at AdventureX 2026: First Prize (Smart Youth Track), Most Shareable Award (Sparkle Track), and Web Application Excellence Award (PICO VR Track). The project explores creative narrative and visual design approaches for virtual museum experiences, integrating interactive storytelling with immersive web technologies.

Introduction:

InteractiMuse is an online virtual museum designed explicitly for children, grounded in the theoretical framework of embodied learning. It integrates immersive interactive experiences with virtual exhibitions and hands-on tasks to facilitate knowledge exploration and deep conceptual understanding. This platform aims to expand the boundaries of virtual museum design by reconfiguring the paradigm of deep learning in museum contexts through the lens of embodied cognition.

Existing offline participatory learning museums fail to deliver fully immersive learning scenarios, as physical exhibition spaces are often constrained by environmental distractions and structural limitations. In contrast, conventional online museums merely transpose offline content into two-dimensional digital formats, lacking dynamic engagement. To address these gaps, InteractiMuse constructs a one-to-one immersive experience interface between users and digital displays. By designing learning-oriented game modules with lightweight accessibility, it makes educational museum experiences accessible to children in diverse settings.

InteractiMuse decomposes complex cultural heritage knowledge into modular, age-appropriate game tasks, while simultaneously fostering children's hand-eye coordination—thus mitigating the prevalent limitation of unidirectional engagement in existing virtual museums. Currently targeted at children aged 6–14, the platform is tailored for family learning contexts, addressing the core challenge that children often struggle with effective learning in traditional museums due to insufficient foundational knowledge of unfamiliar cultural artifacts. Through interactions with InteractiMuse, children not only acquire cultural heritage knowledge but also refine motor skills (e.g., hand-eye coordination), thereby constructing a bodily action-based deep engagement experience that aligns with embodied learning principles.
